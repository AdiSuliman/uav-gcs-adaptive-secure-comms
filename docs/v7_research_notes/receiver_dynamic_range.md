## Q1. IEEE 802.11 "receiver maximum input level" for each PHY

### Takeaway
The 802.11 maximum-input-level requirement is -10 dBm for DSSS/HR-DSSS (CCK 11 Mbit/s, FER 8%). It is -30 dBm for OFDM at 5 GHz (802.11a, PER 10%) and -20 dBm for OFDM at 2.4 GHz (802.11g ERP, PER 10%). According to IEEE 802.11 working-group documents, the -20 dBm (2.4 GHz) and -30 dBm (5 GHz, and 6 GHz from 11ax/11be on) values were kept unchanged for HT (11n), VHT (11ac), HE (11ax), EHT (11be) and the current UHR (11bn) draft text. From HT onward, the level is "measured at each antenna".

### Inferences
- In the 2.4 GHz band, the 802.11 conformance ceiling is -10 dBm for spread-spectrum CCK and -20 dBm for every OFDM-based PHY since 802.11g. At 5 GHz the ceiling is -30 dBm. These are minimum conformance requirements (a PER/FER limit at that level), not damage limits. The 11-21/1563 resolution itself records that "many devices support higher maximum input levels than that required".
- From HT onward, the level is defined per receive antenna ("measured at each antenna"; "average power per receive antenna"), so for a multi-antenna receiver each antenna port is held to the same ceiling.
- The specified 802.11b receive window (-76 dBm sensitivity to -10 dBm max input) spans 66 dB. Hart/Perahia's numbers for 11ax MCS11 (-43 to -30 dBm, 13 dB) and 11be D1 MCS13 (4-10 dB) show the high-order-QAM window is much narrower.

### Gaps
- The approved clause text of IEEE Std 802.11a-1999 (17.3.10.4), 802.11g-2003 (19.5.3) and the HT/VHT/HE receiver-maximum-input-level subclauses of IEEE Std 802.11-2012/2016/2020 was not read. Those documents are not freely downloadable without an IEEE account. The 802.11a and 802.11g values come from R&S and Keysight application notes that quote the clauses. The HT/VHT/HE values come from IEEE 802.11 WG submissions (11-11/1077, 11-21/1563) and the 11bn PDT text.
- No 802.11 document read here specifies the PER at -20 dBm for a single-carrier QPSK 1 Msym/s waveform. The 802.11 requirements are PHY-specific.

## Q2. Maximum input level / P1dB / IIP3 / ADC full-scale / AGC range of SDR transceivers and typical 2.4 GHz link receivers

### Takeaway
At 2.4 GHz and maximum RX gain, the AD9361 datasheet gives IIP3 -14 dBm, NF 3 dB, 73 dB maximum gain, 1 dB gain steps, 100 dB RSSI range and 12-bit ADCs. Its only receiver "maximum" is the absolute-maximum (damage) rating "RF Inputs (Peak Power) 2.5 dBm". The operating table has no maximum-input-level row. The LMS7002M has 12-bit ADCs (0.8 Vpp differential full scale), 70 dB RX gain control range and out-of-band IIP3 of about 4 dBm (datasheet) or 8.8-9.4 dBm (measured at 2.4 GHz). Its RSSI covers "-70 dBm to -20 dBm, corresponding to the full dynamic range of the ADC". Lime measured that the LNA gain must be cut by just over 7 dB to withstand a -15 dBm out-of-band blocker. Simple 2.4 GHz RC/telemetry chips saturate between -18 and -9 dBm (TI CC2500) or tolerate 0 dBm (Nordic nRF24L01+).

### Inferences
- In the AD9361 datasheet, the 2.4 GHz IIP3 (-14 dBm) is specified only at maximum RX gain. The only receiver-input "maximum" is a damage rating (2.5 dBm peak), and no operating maximum-input-level row is given. Any operating ceiling would have to come from the AGC gain table (reference manual, not read) or from the EVM-vs-input-power curves, whose plotted range at 2.4 GHz ends at -25 dBm (Fig. 34).
- The LMS7002M wiki ties the ADC full scale to an RF level: the RF RSSI range of -70 to -20 dBm is described as the "full dynamic range of the ADC" (50 dB).
- The Lime IIP3 rows show IIP3 for out-of-band tones barely changing with LNA gain reduction (8.8 → 9.3-9.4 dBm) while total gain drops by about 29 dB. The datasheet attributes most of the blocker tolerance to the IF low-pass filter rather than to gain reduction.

## Q3. AGC and ADC behaviour when a strong jammer is present (gain reduction, desensitization, quantization, blocking)

### Takeaway
All sources describe the same mechanism. The AGC keeps the ADC input at a fixed level, so a jammer drives the AGC gain down roughly in inverse proportion to total input power (jammer + noise). Ideally, gain × total power = constant. Once the ADC range is set by the jammer, the wanted signal is either clipped (saturation) or buried in quantization noise. Marti et al. prove that for a fixed ADC resolution, the mutual information goes to zero as jammer power grows, whatever AGC strategy is used. Each 6.02 dB of extra jamming needs one more ADC bit, and this limit applies to MIMO spatial nulling done on ADC outputs. Measured numbers read here:
- Lime: noise floor +7-8 dB with a -15.6 dBm CW blocker; about 7 dB of LNA backoff needed for a -15 dBm blocker.
- NavCom GNSS: out-of-band rejection up to +30 dBm; in-band pulse rejection up to -30 dBm; CW loss of lock at J/S 44 dB versus 59 dB depending on the AGC/sampling scheme.
- GNSS detection practice: AGC-drop thresholds of 2 dB (Kazim et al.) and 5 dB (Olsson et al.).

### Inferences
- Olsson et al.'s ideal AGC model (g·P = constant, P ≈ J + N) means that once the jammer exceeds the noise floor at the AGC input, each extra dB of jammer power removes about 1 dB of AGC gain. This is consistent with the stepwise AGC drops Kazim et al. report for 5 dB jammer steps, though Kazim et al. give no slope in numbers. The AGC therefore protects the ADC from clipping but scales the wanted signal down together with the jammer.
- Marti et al. place the limit before any digital combining. Their bound holds "regardless of what kind of automatic gain control is applied" and covers MIMO nulling performed on ADC outputs. By that bound, a digital spatial combiner (such as MMSE) cannot recover what the ADCs have clipped or quantized away. The 6.02 dB/bit rule is their stated intuition, confirmed by their bound.
- Ndili & Enge and NavCom both report that pulsed jammers interact with the AGC time constant. The AGC or adaptive quantizer suppresses pulses, which shows up as a very different AGC signature for pulsed versus continuous jamming. NavCom reports only a duty-cycle-proportional loss with pulse blanking.

- No measured quantization loss in dB (for example for 2-bit or 3-bit ADCs under CW interference) was obtained.
